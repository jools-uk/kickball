How the game flows

The field

Every dimension below is from §2A, drawn to scale.

The shape of a game

  • Seven innings or 45 minutes, whichever comes first. Semi-finals and finals get 50.
  • At the 35-minute mark the head ref announces the final inning. Called in the top of an inning, that inning is the last. Called in the bottom, you get one more full inning.
  • Mercy rule: no more than 6 runs in an inning. The sixth run ends that half-inning on the spot.
  • Slaughter rule: the game ends the moment the run differential passes 12.
  • Home team is decided by rock-paper-scissors; in bracket play the higher seed chooses. Take home — last licks matter in a timed game.

The pitch

  • The pitcher rolls from the pitching mound — a cone or marker, not an actual mound — 42 ft 2.5 in from the back tip of home plate. The ball must be released at the mound, not in front of or beside it.
  • A legal pitch must bounce at least twice before reaching home plate. A smooth roller that doesn't is an automatic ball.
  • The strike zone is the empty space between the two strike plates set either side of home plate: 42 inches wide, 6 inches high. Wide and very low.
  • The zone is judged at the front face of the plate only — unless the ball bounces directly on the plate.

The count

  • Three strikes is an out. Three fouls is an out. At two strikes, the next foul is also an out.
  • Four balls is a walk: the kicker takes first base.
  • Four consecutive balls before any strike or foul is worth two bases. Rare and valuable.
  • If a foul is caused by a fixed obstruction — a tree branch, overhead fencing — you get one extra foul.
A walk only pushes runners who have nowhere else to stand

The kicker takes first, and runners move up only if they're forced out of their base by the person behind them. A runner on first is pushed to second. A runner on second stays put unless first is also occupied. A runner on third only scores if the bases were loaded.

So a walk with a runner on second and nobody on first advances nobody but the kicker. This is why a walk is worth much less with the bases half-empty than it looks.

The kick

  • Contact must happen while the ball is inside the kicker's box (42″ × 48″). The ball's position is what counts, not where your plant foot is.
  • Kicking the ball at or above the knee is a foul.
  • A kick means a leg moving forward. Freeze, and whatever the pitch was stands — so letting it hit your back leg is a strike.
  • Hitting it with an arm, chest, or anything but a leg is a strike.
  • Once the ump makes the call as the ball passes the back of the box, nothing further can be done about it.

Crashing

The defining difference from baseball. Once the ball is pitched, the defense is allowed to run at you.

  • Infielders may start no closer than the 40-foot line — the mark two-thirds of the way down each baseline, with an invisible line joining the two.
  • The pitcher may charge once the ball is pitched, but no closer than 5 feet from the kicker's box. Closer than that before the kick and it's a ball.
  • Fielders may not advance in front of the pitcher. Doing so makes it a ball — unless the kicker chooses to kick it anyway.
  • Everyone must be in position before the kick. After the kick, anyone can go anywhere.
Two things the rulebook gets geometrically wrong here

The crash line sits in front of the pitcher. The 40-ft marks are measured along the baselines, which run at 45° to the home-to-second axis. The line joining them therefore crosses the middle of the diamond about 28 feet from home — roughly 14 feet closer to the kicker than the mound at 42 ft 2.5 in. So a shortstop or charger standing legally on that line is already ahead of the pitcher, which sits awkwardly against the clause forbidding fielders from advancing in front of the pitcher. The only workable reading: that clause governs the charge, not the starting spot — nobody gets closer to home than the pitcher does.

And the note that the mound is "two-thirds the distance of the baseline" doesn't hold. Two-thirds of a 60-ft baseline is 40 ft, measured along a baseline. The mound sits at 42 ft 2.5 in along a different line entirely. Worth agreeing where the cones go before first pitch.

There is no "stay near your position" rule

The only 15 feet in the rulebook is the line defining where the outfield begins — 15 ft behind any base. There's no radius you have to stay within. Fielders just have to be in a legal position before the kick, six in the infield and four in the outfield, and positions lock once the inning starts.

Starting the play

  • The ball is live the moment it leaves the pitcher's hand. That's when the defense may begin crashing.
  • Runners stay frozen a beat longer — you may not leave your base until the ball is kicked.

Running

  • No leading off and no stealing. You cannot step off early for a head start, and you cannot advance during a pitch that isn't kicked.
  • Running through first base, you must touch the orange safety base in foul territory. Missing it is an out.
  • But if you're continuing on to second, you don't need the safety base — the white bag is legal.
  • After overrunning first, turn right. Turning left can be read as intent to advance, and you may be tagged.
  • First base is the only base you may overrun. Anywhere else, stepping off makes you taggable — including when you're forced back to first.
  • Stray more than 5 feet from the baseline — the direct line between the bases — and you're out. That's the rule people run into when dodging a thrown ball.
  • A tie goes to the runner, always.
Nothing in the rulebook covers wet ground

There's no relaxed overrun or sliding allowance for rain or mud. The only weather provision is calling the game: after three full innings it's a regulation result, before that it can be replayed.

How outs are made

  • Force out — a fielder holding the ball touches the base you're obliged to run to, before you get there. They need decisive control of the ball while touching the base.
  • Tag out — a fielder touches you with the ball while you're off a base.
  • Thrown out — a fielder throws the ball at you and hits you at the shoulders or below.
  • Fly out — the ball is caught in the air, in fair or foul territory. A caught foul keeps the ball live, so runners can advance.
  • A ball off your head is a free pass — you're safe. Unless you weren't in running motion, in which case the ump may still call you out.
  • Duck or slide and you forfeit that protection: any part of you becomes fair game.
  • Taking the lead force removes the force from every runner behind it. Get the runner at first and the runner on second is no longer forced.

Ending the play

  • The play is over when the pitcher decisively touches the mound while holding the ball — not when the ball reaches the infield, and not when the ump feels like it.
  • The head ref then calls time and blows a whistle.
  • Runners past halfway to the next base are awarded it. Everyone else returns to their last base.
  • Once the pitcher has touched the mound they can't change their mind and make another play.
Know where halfway is

A runner who is one stride past the midpoint when the pitcher touches the mound gets the base for free. Over a season that's several bases gained by nothing but paying attention.

Kicking

  • Contacting the ball behind the kicker's box — foul.
  • Kicking it at or above the knee — foul. This one quietly stacks up on power kickers who get under the ball.
  • Trapping it at the plate with no forward momentum — foul.
  • Not being there when your spot comes up — out. Know the two names in front of you.

Everything else

Base coaches cannot touch their runners

Touching a runner during live play stops the play and produces an out, with no advancement. Point and shout — never grab, pat, or steer.

  • Bench players stay in the dugout or on-deck box. Touching a ball or blocking a play earns a Yellow Card and kills the play.
  • Pinch runners are not allowed. If you can't kick and run, you can't play.

Winning strategies

Everything on offense flows from one question: how hard is this defense crashing?

Bunt or blast

A bunt is a deliberately soft kick that dies in the infield. A blast is a hard kick meant to get past people. The choice depends almost entirely on where the first and third basemen — the corners — are standing when the ball is pitched.

Bunt
when the corners aren't coming
  • The first and third basemen are still back on the 40-ft line, or standing flat rather than already moving. If nobody is charging, a ball that stops rolling 15 feet down the third-base line takes so long to reach that you'll be standing on first before the throw arrives.
  • You're quick, and either no runners are on, or there's a runner on second only — they aren't forced to move, so your soft kick can't cost them their base.
  • You need a single run and the third baseman is deep. Think about what you're asking of them: sprint 30 feet, pick up a slow-rolling ball barehanded, and make an accurate throw home while your runner on third has been running since contact. That is the hardest sequence in kickball to execute.
  • The defense has just been beaten by a deep kick and has visibly backed everyone up. They're not set to charge.
Kick hard
when they're charging at you
  • They're crashing. A charging infield has nobody behind it — a hard grounder through a gap the fielders have already vacated is a double.
  • Two outs. You want the ball past people rather than riding on one throw.
  • Runners on and you need to move them more than one base.
  • The outfield is shallow, which it usually is, because kickballs don't carry.
Never bunt into the base a forced runner has to reach

A bunt hands the ball to a fielder standing right where the force play is. The two situations to avoid outright:

Bases loaded. Your runner on third is forced home, straight into the fielder who just picked up your bunt. It's the easiest out on the field and often two.

Runners on first and second. A bunt toward third gives the third baseman a force at a base they're already standing on.

With a runner on first only, it's more nuanced than a flat no. A soft bunt down the third-base line can genuinely advance them — the throw to second is long and a kickball rolls slowly. Just know you're trading your own at-bat for their base, so it's a play for a fast lead runner and a slow-footed third baseman, not a default.

Aim, don't just launch

  • Down the lines. Crashing corners leave them open.
  • The third-base side. Longest throw on the field, and the one most likely to sail.
  • The outfield gaps, where two fielders converge and neither calls it.
  • Hard and low at a charging fielder's feet. Nobody fields that cleanly at a sprint.
  • Never the lazy pop-up. It's an out and it freezes every runner. Kick the top half of the ball.
Aim with your hips, not your ankle

Placement comes from your approach, not your foot. Set your run-up so that your hips and plant foot are already pointing where you want the ball to go as you arrive at it. Trying to steer by swivelling your kicking foot at the last moment gives you far less control and usually produces a spinning ball that goes foul or straight up.

Practical version: to go down the third-base line, approach the ball from the first-base side so your body opens that way naturally.

Working the count

  • Don't be afraid to take the first pitch — especially if it arrives uncomfortably fast, bouncy, or with spin on it. You learn the pitcher's delivery and the ump's zone for free. This is what people mean by "wait for yours" and "good eye".
  • A 6-inch zone is low. Most pitchers miss high, and the single fastest improvement available to a new kicker is laying off anything above the shins.
  • Track the double-bounce requirement. Pitchers who put spin on the ball frequently fail to bounce it twice, and each of those is a free ball.
  • Starting 3–0 against a wild pitcher? The two-base award is worth more than most kicks you could produce.
  • With two strikes, a foul ends you. Shorten up, put it on the ground, live to run.
Play the psychological game

If a pitcher has shown you they're struggling, let them keep struggling. Don't rescue them by swinging at something you don't want.

At 3–0 or 3–1, taking a borderline strike costs you very little compared with the value of a walk. A called strike with three balls in hand still leaves you in a good count; a bad kick ends the at-bat entirely. The pitcher feels that asymmetry too, and pitchers who know they have to throw a strike tend to throw a worse one.

Baserunning without a lead

You can't steal and you can't lead off. Your entire edge is anticipation and read speed.

  • Know your halfway mark, because the play dies when the pitcher touches the mound and past halfway you're awarded the base.
  • Make them throw at you. Most fielders miss, and a miss leaves the ball loose with no base cap.
  • Read flies early — see the tag-up note below, because this is the rule most often misapplied.
Tagging up: two separate tests that everyone merges into one

There is no "bobble" or "fumble" rule. Refs get tangled here because two different questions are being decided at once, and they have different answers.

When may you leave? If you stay on your base until a fielder makes first contact with the ball in the air, you may go — and it doesn't matter whether the catch is ultimately completed. Waiting for first contact satisfies tagging up. You do not have to wait for the catch to finish.

Is the kicker out? Separately, the catch has to actually be completed for the fly out to stand. With two outs, if a kicked ball in the air isn't "directly and decisively caught" and a runner scores in the meantime, the run counts.

So the practical instruction is: on any ball in the air, stay put until you see a glove or hand touch it, then run immediately. The word "decisively" is never defined in the rulebook, which is exactly why this gets argued — worth asking your ump how they're reading it.

The overthrow cap depends on the throw, not the runner

The one-base limit applies only to an overthrow to the first baseman for a force out. That runner gets one extra base and no more. Every other runner on the field is unrestricted, so a runner on second scores on an overthrow at first. Round hard on every throw to first.

The cap does not attach to a throw aimed at a runner's body. If a fielder throws at someone and misses, nobody is capped — including the runner heading to first, if that's who they threw at. What matters is whether the throw was to the base for a force, or at a person.

Separately, a ball that leaves the field of play entirely — street, dugout, trash can — caps everyone at two bases, however it got there.

Force outs end innings differently

If the third out is a force, no runs count — even ones that crossed the plate first. If it's a tag, runs scored before the tag do count.

Managing the clock and the run caps

  • Once you've scored five in an inning, run wild. The inning ends at six regardless, so an out costs you almost nothing.
  • Ahead by eleven, play for exactly one run. The twelfth ends the game — don't strand runners chasing a big inning.
  • Trailing when the clock is called: take pitches, use your challenge, extend the inning.
  • Leading when it's called: kick the first decent pitch and keep at-bats short.
  • Your captain's challenge is an asset. It stops the clock for a full minute and you keep it if you win. Save it for a missed base or an inning-ending force.
  • Seeding is settled by record, then head-to-head, then run differential, then total runs. With the inning capped at six, a run you prevent is worth as much as a run you score.

On defense

  • Get the force. It's the only out that doesn't involve touching a moving human.
  • Crash selectively. Corners hard against bunters, middle holds, nobody crashes a power kicker.
  • Throw low — hips and thighs. High is an automatic safe. Never at the head.
  • Get the ball to the pitcher. Ending the play often beats a low-percentage throw.

Where the regular season differs

Everything above is written to the tournament rules. If you're playing a league night, these are the numbers that change.

About the four-foul thing

The tournament PDF says four fouls in §3A but still says three in §4C. The regular season rulebook says three in both places. Play it as three — the tournament document appears to carry an unfinished edit rather than a new rule.

The same pattern shows up in the minimum-player rule: the tournament text says you must begin and finish with eight, then says you forfeit with fewer than seven at start time.

Social (C) versus competitive (A and B)

Only two rules actually differ by division, but the first one reshapes the whole game.

Social — C
underhand, smooth, minimal bounce
  • Pitches must be released underhand at moderate speed with little-to-no bounce. A bouncy pitch is an immediate ball.
  • The catcher cannot advance in front of the kicker before the kick — also a ball.
  • The catcher sets up directly behind the box; side positioning is for intermediate and advanced days only.
Competitive — A and B
two bounces minimum, junk encouraged
  • A pitch that doesn't bounce twice is a ball. Spin and height are weapons.
  • The catcher may set up behind or to the side of the kicker's box.
  • Nothing restricts crashing by division — but a high-bouncing pitch buys the defense an extra beat.
These two pitching rules point opposite ways

§4B makes a pitch that fails to bounce twice a ball. The social-league clause makes a pitch with more than minimal bounce a ball. Read together, a C-division pitcher is asked to do both. In practice the social clause governs on social days — but it's worth confirming, because a strict §4B reading would invert the advice below.

Playing in C: contact rates go up and strikeouts go down, because nobody can throw junk at you. Walks are rarer, so working the count is worth much less — take one pitch to read the ump, then kick. Defense and baserunning decide these games.

Playing in A or B: patience pays, and expect to be jammed by high-bouncing pitches that produce pop-ups.
